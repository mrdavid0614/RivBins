  Qué tenemos que construir

  "Smart Cycle Count Scoring": una app pequeña que ayuda a un equipo de almacén
  a decidir qué ubicaciones (bins) revisar primero en un conteo de inventario.
  Hoy los conteos se reparten al azar. Con la app, cada bin tiene un puntaje de 
  riesgo de 0 a 100 y se revisan primero los que tienen más probabilidad de
  tener errores.

  Stack obligatorio: Next.js (frontend) + NestJS (backend) + Prisma + Postgres.

  Lógica de negocio

  1. El dominio

  - Almacén → Pasillos (aisles) → Racks → Bins (unos 30). Es una jerarquía
    física y define la forma del mapa de calor.
  - Productos que van dentro de pallets, y cada pallet está asignado a un bin.
  - Movimientos de inventario del último mes (simulados en el seed):
    - Putaway: se coloca un pallet en un bin.
    - Pick: se saca producto o un pallet.
    - Adjustment: se corrige la cantidad.
    - Move: un pallet pasa de un bin a otro.

  2. El corazón: el scoring

  Cada bin recibe un score de 0 a 100 calculado a partir de varios factores. El
  PDF sugiere estos:
  - Días desde la última auditoría: cuanto más tiempo sin revisar, más riesgo.
  - Actividad: número de picks, moves y putaways. Más manipulación significa más
    oportunidades de error.
  - Frecuencia de ajustes: si un bin se ajusta seguido, es señal de que el
    sistema y la realidad no coinciden.
  - Otros factores a nuestro criterio. Por ejemplo: resultados de auditorías
    anteriores (fallos recientes), cantidad de pallets o SKUs distintos en el
    bin, o el tamaño de las discrepancias en conteos previos.

  Requisitos clave:
  - Guardar el score y el desglose por factor, porque la UI tiene que explicar
    "por qué" un bin tiene ese puntaje (explicabilidad).
  - Un endpoint o botón para recalcular los scores.
  - En el README hay que documentar los factores y cómo se combinan (por
    ejemplo, normalizar cada factor a 0–1 y hacer una suma ponderada × 100).

  3. El ciclo que "aprende"

  Esta es la idea central del negocio:

  Score → Heatmap → Plan de auditoría (Top N) → Conteo (pass/fail) → Recompute →
  Nuevo score

  "El sistema se vuelve más inteligente" significa, en la práctica, que los 
  resultados de las auditorías alimentan el siguiente cálculo:
  - Un bin auditado y que pasa vuelve a "días desde la última auditoría ≈ 0",
    así que su score baja.
  - Un bin que falla debería subir de riesgo, o al menos no bajar tanto, porque
    el historial de fallos es un factor.

  No es machine learning. Es retroalimentación sobre factores deterministas.

  4. Plan de auditoría y tareas

  - El usuario pulsa "Generate Audit Plan" y obtiene los N bins con mayor score.
  - Esos bins se guardan como tareas con estado PENDING o DONE.
  - Hay una vista de tabla con las tareas.

  5. Flujo de conteo (mobile-friendly)

  - Buscar o "escanear" el código del bin.
  - Ver los pallets esperados en ese bin, según el sistema.
  - Ingresar la cantidad contada y marcar pass/fail.
  - Al guardar: se registra la auditoría, la tarea pasa a DONE y se dispara un 
    recompute del score.

  Pantallas del MVP

  1. Dashboard / Heatmap: una grilla por pasillo y rack, con colores de verde a
     amarillo a rojo según el score.
  2. Detalle del bin (drawer o página): score actual, desglose del "por qué",
     fecha de la última auditoría y pallets actuales.
  3. Plan de auditoría y tareas: el botón de Top N y la tabla de tareas.
  4. Conteo móvil: búsqueda del bin, lo esperado, lo contado y pass/fail.

  Entregables

  - La app funcionando (Next.js + NestJS) con schema, seed, servicio de scoring,
    heatmap con detalle, plan y tareas, y el conteo móvil.
  - Un README con instalación, seed y ejecución, más la explicación de los
    factores de scoring y de cómo se combinan.

  Puntos ambiguos que tendremos que decidir

  1. Pass/fail: ¿lo marca el usuario a mano o se calcula comparando la cantidad
     contada con la esperada (con alguna tolerancia)? El PDF dice ambas cosas.
     Yo calcularía una sugerencia automática y dejaría que el usuario la
     confirme.
  2. ¿Qué se cuenta?: ¿una cantidad total por bin, por pallet o por producto?
     Esto define el modelo de AuditResult.
  3. ¿Un fallo genera un ajuste?: si el conteo no coincide, ¿se crea un
     movimiento ADJUSTMENT que actualiza el inventario, o solo se registra la
     discrepancia?
  4. Recompute: ¿se recalculan todos los bins o solo el auditado? Con unos 30
     bins, recalcular todos es trivial y más simple.
  5. Valor de N en el Top N: ¿configurable desde la UI? Y si ya hay tareas
     PENDING, ¿un nuevo plan las reemplaza, se acumulan o se excluyen esos bins?
  6. Normalización del score: ¿relativa a los demás bins (el más activo = 1) o
     con umbrales absolutos? La relativa garantiza que el heatmap siempre
     muestre contraste.

  Cuando quieras empezar, puedo proponerte el modelo de datos (schema de Prisma)
  y la fórmula de scoring antes de escribir código.

  2. What does the counter count?

  You confirmed a pallet holds several products, so a bin looks like this:

  Bin A-01-03
  ├── Pallet P-100
  │   ├── Product X — expected 40 units
  │   └── Product Y — expected 15 units
  └── Pallet P-101
      └── Product Z — expected 60 units

  There are three ways to record the count:

  Option: A. Per bin
  User enters: One total (115)
  Pros: Fastest
  Cons: Misses errors that cancel out (X is 5 short, Z is 5 over → total still
  matches). Can't tell which product is wrong.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Option: B. Per pallet
  User enters: One total per pallet (55, 60)
  Pros: Middle ground
  Cons: Still can't tell which product is wrong.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Option: C. Per product line in each pallet
  User enters: 40, 15, 60
  Pros: Most accurate. Shows exactly where the discrepancy is, and pass/fail is
  meaningful.
  Cons: More fields. Still fine on mobile with about 2–5 lines per bin.

  Recommendation: C. Real cycle counts work this way. It also gives the score
  better data, such as how large the discrepancy was. The bin fails if any line
  falls outside tolerance.

  3. What happens when a count fails?

  Say the system expects 40 units of X and the counter finds 35. Should the
  inventory record change?

  - Option A: record only. We save the discrepancy (expected 40, counted 35).
    The system still says 40. The next count compares against 40 again, so the
    bin keeps failing until someone fixes it some other way.
  - Option B: record and correct. We save the discrepancy and also create an
    ADJUSTMENT movement (−5). The pallet line becomes 35, so the system now
    matches reality.

  Recommendation: B. That's what a cycle count is for. It also makes the
  feedback loop consistent: the adjustment raises the adjustment-frequency
  factor, the failure raises the failure-history factor, and the next count
  compares against corrected data. The manual override from your answer to #1
  also applies here. If the user overrides to pass, we record the difference but
  don't create an adjustment.

  6. How is each factor turned into a 0–1 value?

  Before the factors can be combined, each one has to be on the same scale. Take
  "activity in the last 30 days" as the example: bin A has 50 movements, bin B
  has 10, and the busiest bin has 80.

  - Option A: relative normalization. Each bin is compared with the others. The
    busiest bin = 1.0, so A = 50/80 = 0.63 and B = 10/80 = 0.13.
    - Pro: the heatmap always shows contrast.
    - Con: a bin's score depends on the other bins. When one bin changes, the
      maximum can change, and every other score moves with it.
  - Option B: fixed thresholds. Each factor has a set ceiling, for example "60+
    movements = maximum risk". Then A = 50/60 = 0.83 and B = 10/60 = 0.17.
    - Pro: a bin's score depends only on its own data. It's stable and easy to
      explain ("this bin has 50 moves; 60 or more is the maximum").
    - Con: the thresholds have to be chosen, and if every bin is quiet, the
      whole heatmap is green.

  Recommendation: B (fixed thresholds). This is directly tied to your answer on
  #4. You chose to recompute only the audited bin. With relative normalization,
  that would leave the other bins with stale scores whenever the maximum
  changes. Fixed thresholds make per-bin recompute correct. We'll document the
  thresholds in the README and seed data that produces a good spread of colors.

  Warehouse → Aisle → Rack → Bin

  total.

  | Type         | Meaning

         |
  | `ADJUSTMENT` | Quantity of a pallet line corrected (manual or from a failed
  count)  |

  distribution
  across bins so the scores spread across green, yellow, and red.

  ### Audits

  Top N riskiest

    - Bin-level: auto-computed pass/fail, the user's final pass/fail (override),

  bin can
  be recomputed on its own.

  |---|-------------------------------|-----------------------------------------

  - **Manual "Recompute scores" action** (button + endpoint): recomputes **all

  - **Automatic after an audit result is saved:** recomputes **only the audited

  Score → Heatmap → Audit Plan (Top N) → Count → Recompute → New score

  - A **passed** audit resets "time since last audit", so the score drops.
  - A **failed** audit raises failure history, and the correction raises
  adjustment frequency.
  - This is deterministic feedback, **not machine learning**.

  ## Count Logic

  ### Granularity
  The user counts **each product line on each pallet** in the bin.

  ### Pass/fail: automatic with manual override
  1. **Auto result:** the bin passes only if **every** pallet line matches its
  expected
     quantity (tolerance: *proposed* exact match, 0 units, configurable).
     Any line outside tolerance means the bin fails.
  2. **Override:** the user can flip the auto result before saving. The app
  stores both the
     auto and the final result.

  ### Correcting inventory
  - **Final result = FAIL:** for each mismatched line, create an `ADJUSTMENT`
  movement and
    update the pallet line quantity to the counted value. System inventory then
  matches reality.
  - **Final result = PASS** (including an override from fail): record any
  differences but
    **do not** create adjustments or change inventory.

  ### On save
  1. Persist the AuditResult (lines + auto/final result).
  2. Apply adjustments if the final result is FAIL.
  3. Update the bin's last audit date.
  4. Mark the bin's `PENDING` task as `DONE`, if one exists.
  5. Recompute that bin's score.

  ## Scoring System (core business logic)

  Each bin gets a **score from 0 to 100**. A higher score means the bin is
  riskier.

  ### Scoring window: "since last audit"
  Activity-based factors count only movements **since the bin's last audit**,
  because
  movements before that were already verified by the count.
  - **Never-audited bin:** use the bin's full movement history (the seeded 30
  days).
  - **Adjustments created by an audit** (failed count corrections) are
  **excluded** from
    the adjustment factor. The audit's outcome is already captured by factors 4
  and 5.

  ### Factors
  Each factor is normalized to **0–1 using fixed thresholds** (absolute, not
  relative to
  other bins). This makes a bin's score depend only on its own data, so a single
  bin can
  be recomputed on its own.

  `normalized = min(rawValue / threshold, 1)` (unless noted otherwise)

  | # | Factor                    | Raw value
                                | Threshold (proposed) | Weight (proposed) |
  |---|---------------------------|---------------------------------------------
  -------------------------------|----------------------|-------------------|
  | 1 | Time since last audit     | Days since last audit (never audited =
  threshold)                          | 30 days              | 0.25
  |
  | 2 | Activity since last audit | `PICK` + `MOVE` + `PUTAWAY` count since last
  audit                         | 60 movements         | 0.20              |
  | 3 | Adjustments since last audit | Manual `ADJUSTMENT` count since last
  audit                              | 5 adjustments        | 0.15
  |
  | 4 | Audit failure history     | Failed audits in the last 90 days (does
  **not** reset on audit)            | 2 failures           | 0.15
  |
  | 5 | Last discrepancy size     | `Σ|counted − expected| / Σ expected` from
  the last audit (never audited = 0) | 20%                  | 0.15
  |
  | 6 | SKU mix                   | Distinct products currently in the bin
                                | 6 SKUs               | 0.10              |

  **SKU mix normalization:** `min((distinctSkus − 1) / (threshold − 1), 1)`, so
  a
  single-SKU bin scores 0 and a bin with 6 or more SKUs scores 1. An empty bin
  also scores 0.

  ### Combination
  `score = round(Σ (normalized_i × weight_i) × 100)`. The weights sum to 1.0.

  Thresholds and weights live in one config/constants file and are documented in
  the README.

  ### Explainability (required)
  For every bin, persist the **score plus a per-factor breakdown**: raw value,
  threshold,
  normalized value, weight, and contribution in points. The UI uses this to show
  **why** a
  bin has its score.

  ### Recompute
  - **Manual "Recompute scores" action** (button + endpoint): recomputes **all 
  bins**.
  - **Automatic after an audit result is saved:** recomputes **only the audited 
  bin**.

  ### Feedback loop ("the system gets smarter")
  Score → Heatmap → Audit Plan (Top N) → Count → Recompute → New score

  - **Any audit** resets factors 1, 2, and 3 (time, activity, and adjustments
  start
    counting from zero).
  - A **passed** audit sets factor 5 (discrepancy) to around 0, so the score
  drops sharply.
  - A **failed** audit raises factor 4 (failure history), and factor 5 reflects
  how far off
    the count was. A bin that was badly off stays risky even right after it was
  counted.
  - This is deterministic feedback, **not machine learning**.

  I made a few choices you may want to check:

  1. Adjustments created by an audit don't count toward factor 3. Otherwise a
     failed count would be punished twice, through factors 3 and 4/5. Earlier I
     said the correction raises adjustment frequency. I removed that.
  2. Failure history keeps a fixed 90-day window and does not reset when a bin 
     is audited. This is how a bin's track record survives a new audit.
  3. Factor 5 measures the discrepancy before the override, using the actual
     counted vs. expected values. So if the user overrides a small mismatch to
     PASS, the discrepancy still counts, but no adjustment is created.

  To support factor 5, AuditResult will also store the bin-level discrepancy
  ratio.

⏺ 2. Which result feeds factor 4 (failure history)

  Every audit stores two results:
  - autoOutcome: what the system calculated by comparing counted vs. expected
    quantities.
  - finalOutcome: what the user saved, which is the auto result or the user's
    override.

  Factor 4 counts failed audits, so we have to choose which of the two it reads.
  Factor 5 doesn't have this issue because it always uses the real counted
  numbers.

  The difference only shows up when the user overrides the result:

  Case A: auto FAIL, user overrides to PASS
  The system expected 200 units and the counter found 199. The counter knows a
  unit is set aside for a damaged-goods return, so they mark it PASS.

  Recommendation: finalOutcome. Case B is the deciding argument. If factor 4
  reads autoOutcome, a failure the user spotted (a wrong label or damaged
  product, not a count mismatch) never affects the score. The risk with
  finalOutcome is that someone overrides real mismatches to PASS. Factor 5
  limits that risk, because it always records the real difference, and both
  results are stored, so overrides can be checked later.

  3. Score history

  The model changes from one score per bin to an append-only history, with a
  pointer from each bin to its current score:

  prisma
  enum ScoreTrigger {
    SEED              // initial computation after seeding
    MANUAL_RECOMPUTE  // "Recompute scores" button (all bins)
    AUDIT             // automatic recompute after a count
  }

  model Bin {
    // ...existing fields
    currentScoreId Int?      @unique
    currentScore   BinScore? @relation("CurrentScore", fields: [currentScoreId],
  references: [id])
    scoreHistory   BinScore[] @relation("ScoreHistory")
  }

  model BinScore {
    id            Int          @id @default(autoincrement())
    binId         Int
    score         Int          // 0–100
    factors       Json         // FactorBreakdown[]: raw, threshold, normalized,
  weight, points
    trigger       ScoreTrigger
    auditResultId Int?         // set when trigger = AUDIT
    computedAt    DateTime     @default(now())

    bin           Bin          @relation("ScoreHistory", fields: [binId],
  references: [id])
    currentFor    Bin?         @relation("CurrentScore")
    auditResult   AuditResult? @relation(fields: [auditResultId], references:
  [id])

    @@index([binId, computedAt])
  }

  Why it's shaped this way:
  - Rows are never updated. Each recompute inserts a new row and moves
    Bin.currentScoreId to it, in one transaction.
  - Each row explains itself. The factors JSON already stores the threshold and
    weight used. If the config changes later, old scores still show exactly how
    they were calculated.
  - trigger + auditResultId record why the score changed. For example: "dropped
    from 78 to 22 after audit #14".
  - The heatmap and Top N stay simple because they read through currentScore.
    There's no need for a "latest row per bin" query.
  - It enables a UI extra: a small score timeline in the Bin Detail drawer. It's
    optional, but cheap with this model.

  4. Package manager alternatives

  pnpm
  - ✅ Fastest installs and least disk space.
  - ✅ Strict: you can't import a package you didn't declare.
  - ✅ The most common choice for TS monorepos.
  - ⚠️ The reviewer may not have it installed. corepack enable plus a
    packageManager field in package.json fixes that with one command.

  npm workspaces
  - ✅ No extra install, since it comes with Node.
  - ✅ Every reviewer can run it immediately.
  - ⚠️ Slower than pnpm.
  - ⚠️ Loose: a package can import dependencies it never declared and still work
    by accident.
  - ⚠️ Fewer workspace features (filtering, running scripts in parallel).

  Yarn (Berry, v4)
  - ✅ Fast, with good workspace commands.
  - ⚠️ Its default dependency mode (Plug'n'Play) causes friction with Prisma,
    NestJS, and some editors. You usually switch it back to node_modules mode,
    which removes much of its advantage.
  - ⚠️ Also needs corepack.

  Bun workspaces
  - ✅ Extremely fast.
  - ⚠️ Compatibility with NestJS (decorators and reflection) and Prisma tooling
    is less proven.
  - ⚠️ It's a risk for a test where "it runs on the reviewer's machine" is the
    top priority.

  Two independent projects (no workspace)
  - ✅ The simplest setup.
  - ⚠️ Shared types get duplicated or drift apart.
  - ⚠️ Two installs and two sets of scripts.

  Turborepo / Nx
  - These are not alternatives to the above. They're task runners that sit on
    top of any package manager and add caching and task ordering.
  - ⚠️ Overkill for two apps and one shared package.

  Recommendation: still pnpm, pinned through the packageManager field so
  corepack enable is the only setup step. Its strictness catches
  undeclared-dependency mistakes, and it's the standard choice for TS monorepos.

  npm workspaces is the safe fallback. If you want zero setup friction for the
  reviewer, it's a perfectly fine choice. In a repo this small, pnpm's speed and
  disk savings barely matter. The real trade-off is pnpm's strictness against
  npm's zero setup.
